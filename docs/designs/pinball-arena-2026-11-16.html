Pinball Arena

The game design for a two-player browser pinball game with a cogent-built table: how it looks, feels and behaves.

Executive Summary

Pinball Arena is a two-player pinball game that runs in a web browser. The Flipper player is a person at the page (keyboard or gamepad) who plays pinball: two flippers (the "paddles" of the request), a plunger and nudges. The Arena player is the person's own cogent, their Softmax AI agent. The cogent writes BASIC programs that build and rebuild the table while the ball is in play. The programs also define the rules of the game as small state machines, and table events trigger them (a bumper is hit, a flipper goes up, a ball drains). The Arena player's aim is to make the Flipper player have fun and get better at pinball. All score comes from the Arena player's rules.

The page sits next to a chat panel where the person talks to their cogent. The cogent sends new table programs to the page, the page reports back how each Episode (one game of three balls) went, and the cogent revises. The game looks and sounds like a mid-1990s pre-rendered space-themed PC pinball table: one fixed view, a side panel with score and short capital-letter messages, chrome, neon lights and a dark starfield. It is an original table in that style. Where possible, its art and sound are CC0 (a public-domain dedication), and it copies nothing from any existing game. The game is hosted as a public website, not on Softmax's game infrastructure.

1 · The two players
  • The Flipper player is a person in the browser. The Arena player is a BASIC script from that person's cogent (their Softmax AI agent).
  • The Arena player's aim is to make the Flipper player have fun and improve, not to beat the Flipper player.

The Flipper player is a person in the browser. A BASIC script can also drive the flippers. The bundled demo player (attract mode) and the tests use such a script.

The Arena player is a BASIC script. It comes from the person's cogent through the cogent connection, the link between the page and the cogent (section 7). When nobody is signed in, it is the bundled house script.

The whole game runs in the browser, so flipper timing never depends on the network.

If the Arena script fails or overruns its BASIC budget (Appendix C), the table freezes as it is and the existing rules keep running. The game reports the failure to the cogent. If a Flipper script fails, the flippers go limp.

2 · The table
  • Three levels, and the ball always rolls on a surface.
  • Ball physics is new, exact fixed-point work.

The playfield is portrait with real-machine proportions: about 20 x 42 in, sloped about 6.5 degrees toward the player, with a 1-1/16 in steel ball.1113

Some parts are fixed, such as the walls and the flippers. Nothing can be built in the keep-out zones around the flippers, the drain (where balls leave play) and the shooter lane (where the plunger launches each ball) (Appendix G). Everything else is buildable.

The table has three levels. Level 1 is the playfield surface, and levels 2 and 3 are raised. The ball always rolls on a surface (no airborne balls), and it only touches components and other balls on its own level. Raised levels hold only track and the small things that sit on track: rollover switches, gates, spinners and lights. Up to 6 balls can be in play at once (multiball).

Ball and flipper feel

The ball must feel heavy and fast like a real steel ball. The fidelity target is that real techniques work, such as the cradle (a ball that rests on a held flipper) and the live catch (a timed flip that stops the ball dead).11 The control rules are in Appendix G.

Polyworld (the game engine that this game is built on) has no physics engine, only flat circle collision, so ball physics is new work.1 It must be exact, repeatable fixed-point maths so that replays match. This is the largest build risk. The game runs at 120 steps per second, so a flipper press takes effect within about 8 ms.

3 · Episode flow
  • An Episode is a build phase, then three balls.
  • Two starting tables exist: the Blank table and the House table.
  1. Build phase. Before the first ball, the Arena player gets a short window (default 10 s of game time) to build with no edit-rate limit, inside the build budget.
  2. Three balls. The Flipper player plunges each ball. A ball that drains within a few seconds of launch is returned (ball save).
  3. End. The Episode ends when the third ball drains or the game clock reaches 8 minutes, whichever is first. The clock exists because the Arena player could build a table that never drains.
  4. After the Episode. The page shows the score. If a cogent is connected, the page sends it a summary of the Episode.

A "ball search" frees a ball that is stuck (Appendix G).

On the "Blank table", the Arena player starts from nothing. On the "House table", it starts from a bundled standard layout and rule set, which it can change. A bundled house Arena script and a bundled demo Flipper script ship with the game.

4 · What the Arena player can build
  • The Arena player builds from seven component families, inside a build budget.
  • Each edit appears first as a hologram, then turns solid.

Every component has a position, a rotation (any angle), a size, a level, an optional group tag, and type-specific settings. The group tag makes banks and lets one rule address several parts. The families are bumpers, targets, holes and launchers, magnets, flow control, track, and lights and text (Appendix A). The toggle target, which flips between two faces on each hit, is our reading of "flip targets".

Track

Track is two mouth pieces (entry and exit pieces) connected by a curve (Figure 3). A mouth piece has a width at each of its two ends, so it can be a funnel or a spreader. Each mouth sits on one of the three levels. Mouths on different levels make a ramp. Mouths on the same raised level make a rail (a wireform, in pinball terms), and mouths on level 1 make a walled lane (more in Appendix G).

Build budget

The build budget caps how much the Arena player can have on the table at once. Each component type has a cost. A placed piece spends its cost, and a deleted piece gives the cost back. The table total is 100 by default. The game rejects an edit that would go over the total.

Costs follow how much a piece can do to the ball: a wall or a rubber post costs 1, a pop bumper 4, and a magnet 6. Lamps and text are free. Size does not change cost. Full costs are in Appendix A. A cap of 128 pieces also applies, and it counts the free ones.

Editing rules: how "at will" works

New and moved pieces beam in: a translucent hologram shows for about half a second and then turns solid. A hologram with a ball inside it waits until the ball is clear, so the Flipper player can always see a change before it arrives.

A moved piece fades out and beams in at the new place. Solid pieces never slide, so the Arena player cannot shove the ball with a wall: magnets and kickers are the sanctioned ways to push it. A deleted piece that holds a ball releases the ball.

A rejected edit (keep-out zone, over budget, overlap) does nothing, and the game reports it to the Arena player.

The limits (beam-in time, edit rate, budget, piece cap, build-phase length) are table settings. They have defaults (Appendix C), and each can be changed per table.

5 · Rules: rewards and quests as state machines
  • Edges name events, and nodes carry results.
  • Only results award or show anything.

A rule is a finite state machine (FSM): nodes joined by edges, with one current node. It has one initial node, and the Arena player can have many FSMs at once (limits in Appendix C).

An edge goes from one node to another and names the event that fires it (Appendix B). It can narrow the event to a specific component or group tag, a level, a minimum hit strength, and a count ("on the 3rd hit"). An edge can also be a timeout ("after 10 s in this node").

A node carries results that happen when the FSM enters it. The result kinds are score changes, sounds and visuals from fixed libraries, on-screen text, arena reconfiguration, ball actions, and signals to other FSMs (Appendix G).

A node with no outgoing edges returns to the initial node straight after its results run. The simplest rule thus repeats on every hit: one edge, "pop bumper hit", leads to a node with the results "+5 score, ping". Figure 2 shows a quest with a time limit. The evaluation order is fixed, and rules cannot loop within one tick.

Results are the only way anything is awarded or shown. The Arena script cannot add score directly, but it can raise a "script signal" that an FSM listens for. This keeps one place where rewards live, and it makes replays complete. Score changes can add or subtract, and the Flipper score never goes below 0. Reconfigurations from a node obey the same editing rules as direct edits.

6 · What each player sees and does
  • The Flipper player sees the table but not the FSMs. The Arena player sees both.
  • A person plays with a keyboard or a gamepad. Scripts run in a BASIC virtual machine.

Figure 4 gives the short form of each player's observations and actions (full lists in Appendix G). The Flipper player cannot see the FSMs. As on a real machine, the player learns the rules from lamps, messages and results.

A person plays in the browser, with a keyboard or a gamepad. Gamepad input uses paddy, a gamepad library by the author of Polyworld that works in browsers.19 The control table is in Appendix G.

BASIC scripts run on Bassy, the BASIC virtual machine that every Polyworld game uses.2 The Flipper script decides 60 times per second and the Arena script 12 times per second.

7 · The cogent connection
  • The Arena player is the person's own cogent, their Softmax AI agent.
  • The loop is chat, new table program, play, report, revise.

A person can play without signing in. The house Arena script then runs the table. Signing in with a Softmax account connects the page to the person's cogent. A chat panel opens beside the table. The person can talk to the cogent in plain words ("more ramps", "that magnet is unfair", "teach me to cradle").

The cogent writes an Arena program in BASIC and sends it to the page. The page shows a notice ("NEW TABLE PROGRAM RECEIVED"), stores the program under its name, and loads it. The person does not approve each program. The program itself says when it takes effect: at the start of the next Episode (the default), at the next ball, or at once, with every piece beaming in. A "restart now" button starts a new Episode on the new program. Stored programs appear in a short "table programs" list, so the person can go back to an earlier one.

After each Episode the page sends the cogent a summary:

  • The score.
  • How long each ball lasted.
  • Which components were hit and how often.
  • Which rules fired.
  • Rejected edits.
  • Any script error, with the script's last printed lines.

The cogent can use this summary to revise the program. What the cogent learns from the summary, and when it revises, is outside this design. The task of the page is to follow the AIDE contract described below. For now the cogent does not see the live game. It sees these summaries and what the person says. Live observation and live control by the cogent stay possible later, most likely through the surface messages of the contract (surface.event and surface.act). The cogent learns the Arena BASIC interface from a guide that the page supplies with its messages. If the cogent is asleep, busy, out of model budget or disconnected, the game keeps playing with the current program, and the chat panel says what is happening.

AIDE is the Softmax interface that a web page uses to talk to a cogent's workspace. The page follows AIDE version 1.0.0, the published contract between web pages and cogents.2021 The compliance list is Appendix D.

One rule needs a reading. The contract says that a page deploys a policy (here, the Arena program) and "never runs it in the page". The game runs the program inside its BASIC virtual machine, which has no access to the page, its storage, its cookies or the network. The owners of the contract must confirm this reading (a risk in section 11).

8 · Look and sound
  • An original table in the style of mid-1990s pre-rendered space pinball.
  • One fixed view of the whole table, with a chat panel beside it.

The reference is mid-1990s pre-rendered 3D PC pinball with a space theme.14 We take the general look and feel only. The table layout and all names are original. Art and sound come from CC0 libraries where they fit, and we make the rest new. Nothing comes from the reference game or from any commercial table. A licence record kept with the game lists every asset (name, author, link, licence, date fetched). Candidates are in Appendix F. The successor of the reference game's publisher holds the rights and appears to enforce them.15

One fixed camera looks from the player's end, slightly above. The whole table is always visible, with no scrolling or camera moves (Figure 1). The game renders at modern resolution and scales to fit.

The table and its side panel keep the classic 4:3 composition: the table takes about two thirds and the side panel one third. The chat panel is a third column to the right of that block. It can be collapsed, and on a narrow screen it becomes a drawer.

The playfield is a deep navy and purple starfield with chrome rails and glowing lamps. The finish is soft, airbrushed and slightly dithered, as if from a 256-colour pre-rendered sprite. Polyworld's normal look is toon-shaded low-poly, so this needs its own material and lighting treatment (Appendix G).

Because the table changes, the playfield artwork is a generic starfield, and component identity comes from the pieces themselves. Lamps are the main way the rules talk to the Flipper player.

Sound is short sampled effects from a fixed library, plus optional looping music that is off by default. Sounds come from CC0 packs and CC0 recordings of real machines (Appendix F). Sound never affects the game, because it comes after what is drawn.9

Replays show the same view. Text from Arena programs is limited in characters and length (Appendix C). Only the person whose cogent wrote the text sees it.

9 · Scoring
  • All score comes from FSM nodes, the Arena player's rules.
  • The Arena player has no score yet, and the game has no ranking.

The table awards nothing by itself, so an Arena program with no rules gives a score of 0.

The Flipper score is the sum of score changes. Rules can add or subtract. One change is at most 1,000,000 in size. The Flipper score never goes below 0.

The Arena player has no score yet. How to score the Arena player is undecided, and the page shows only the Flipper score. Because the Arena player sets every reward, scores from different table programs are not meant to be compared. The game has no ranking.

10 · Alternatives considered
  • The game runs in the browser, not on a server.
  • The cogent sends programs and does not control the table live.
  1. Built-in scores on components (a bumper is always worth something), or FSM-only scoring. The design is FSM-only. It matches the request, gives one place where rewards live, and the house rule set provides normal pinball scoring for anyone who wants it. The cost is that an empty rule set is a dull game.
  2. When edits can happen. (a) Instantly, (b) only between balls, or (c) any time, with beam-in delay, edit rate and budget. The design uses (c). Instant edits let the Arena player teleport a wall onto the ball's path with no chance to react, and they break the ball physics. Between-balls-only loses the live "reconfigure at will" idea.
  3. Sliding solid pieces, or fade-and-beam-in moves. The design uses beam-in. Sliding walls can push or crush the ball and are hard to make feel fair.
  4. Full 3D ball flight, or a ball always on a surface with three levels. The design is surface-only. It matches the request (track at three levels), keeps the fixed view readable, and keeps the physics tractable. The cost is no airballs or jumps.
  5. A scrolling or free camera, or one fixed view. The design uses the fixed view, which is the core of the reference look and keeps a changing table readable.
  6. Score direct from the Arena script, or only through FSMs. The design uses only FSMs, and script signals bridge the gap.
  7. The game on a server, or the game in the browser. The design runs the game in the browser. Flipper timing must not wait on a network, and a static site is simpler to host. The cost is that the Arena program runs on the player's machine, so it must be sandboxed. The BASIC virtual machine is that sandbox.
  8. Live control of the table by the cogent, or programs from the cogent. The design uses programs for now. A model cannot decide 12 times per second, and a program keeps playing when the cogent is asleep. The cost is that the cogent only learns between Episodes. Live observation and control stay possible later through the surface messages.

11 · Risks and validation
  • Ball physics is new and carries the whole feel of the game.
  • The cogent connection needs Softmax access that an outside site does not have by default.

Risks:

  • Ball physics is new, must be exact fixed-point maths, and carries the whole feel of the game.
  • The cogent connection depends on Softmax access that an outside site does not have by default. The site's address must be added to Softmax's list of allowed cogent sites. Sign-in gives an account-wide credential that is meant for sites that Softmax controls.2223
  • The "never runs it in the page" rule needs confirmation for a sandboxed BASIC program.20
  • The policy and surface messages are new in the contract, and no existing page implements them yet.21 The cogent side must also learn the Arena guide.
  • The 1990s pre-rendered look is far from Polyworld's toon style.
  • A changing table can produce unfair or unplayable states. Keep-out zones, beam-in, ball search and the clock are the guards, and they need playtests.
  • No CC0 plunger-spring or spinner sound was found, and no CC0 3D pinball parts exist. Those are made new (Appendix F).

The design works when:

  • A person can cradle, live-catch and post-pass on the House table. (A post pass bounces the ball off a post to the other flipper.)
  • A 20-line BASIC Arena program can place a bumper and make it score.
  • The quest in Figure 2 can be written, and it reads clearly from lamps and messages alone.
  • A signed-in person asks the cogent for a change in chat, and the next Episode plays on the changed table.
  • A replay of an Episode matches the original.

Appendix E · Hosting and platform facts

The design relies on these hosting and platform facts:

  • Hosting. A public site on Vercel. The game is a static browser build of the Polyworld fork (WebAssembly). A small server part handles Softmax sign-in and passes the cogent connection through, because a browser cannot attach the sign-in credential to a WebSocket by itself22. Vercel functions support WebSockets and close them when the function reaches its time limit. The page thus reconnects and rereads, as the contract already requires24.
  • Softmax side. The site's address must be on Softmax's list of allowed cogent sites. The credential is account-wide and long-lived, and it is kept in an encrypted cookie that page code cannot read2223.
  • Signed-out play. It needs none of this.
  • Polyworld. A Nim engine with low-poly 3D that runs native and in the browser. Replays re-simulate from recorded actions and check a state hash each tick, so the simulation must be exact1. Read at commit be1706dc. The game is a standalone fork, as Paintbot is.
  • Bassy BASIC. The BASIC virtual machine that every Polyworld game uses: a QBasic-like dialect with integer and fixed-point numbers. The script runs once per decision, its variables persist, and each decision has a fixed instruction budget. The game provides the observation records and the action calls. No floats, and no built-in maths or random numbers (the game supplies them). PRINT goes to a private per-seat log2.
  • Gamepad. The paddy library, with native and browser back ends, under the MIT licence19.
  • Audio. Sound follows what is drawn and never affects the game9.
  • Contract versions read. AIDE 1.0.0, as published at softmax.com/docs/aide.

Appendix G · Detail lists

These lists give the full detail behind the short forms in the body.

Fixed parts and keep-out zones

  • Fixed parts that the Arena player cannot change: the outer walls and top arch, the two flippers, the inlanes and outlanes (the lanes beside the flippers), the drain, the apron (the cover below the flippers), and the shooter lane with its plunger on the right.
  • Keep-out zones, where nothing can be built: the area that the flippers sweep plus a margin, the drain mouth, and the shooter lane.

Script failure

  • If the Arena script fails or overruns its budget, the table freezes as it is, and the existing rules keep running. The game reports the failure to the cogent.
  • If a Flipper script fails, the flippers go limp.

Stuck balls

  • If a ball produces no event and barely moves for several seconds, the table runs a "ball search": it fires kickers and pulses magnets. If that fails, the ball is moved back to the plunger.

Ball feel and controls

  • Ball: it accelerates down the slope, rebounds crisply off rubber, dies on dead surfaces, and can spin off a flipper tip.
  • Flippers: flip (fast full stroke), hold (stays up while pressed), release (drops).
  • Techniques that must work (source 11 and research notes)11: cradle (a ball resting on a held flipper), live catch (a timed flip that stops the ball dead), drop catch (a timed release that does the same), post pass (a bounce off a post that passes the ball to the other flipper).
  • Plunger: hold to pull back, release to fire. The pull length sets the launch strength.
  • Nudge: left, right, up. Each nudge shoves the balls slightly. Two warnings, then tilt: the flippers go dead until the ball drains. Tilt is an event that the Arena player's rules can react to.

Track rules

  • The curve between two mouths is a smooth path shaped by control points. Its width blends from one mouth's throat width to the other's.
  • A track between mouths on different levels is a ramp. The ball needs enough speed to climb it and rolls back if it does not.
  • Tracks on different levels may cross. Tracks on the same level may not overlap.
  • A ball on a raised track stays on it until it leaves through a mouth. Dropping out of a raised mouth lands the ball on the level below at that spot.
  • Events: track entered, track completed (in at one mouth, out at the other), track failed (rolled back).

Rule evaluation order

  • When one event matches several FSMs, each advances once, in the order the FSMs were created.
  • Inside one FSM the first matching edge wins.
  • Signals raised by a node are delivered on the next tick, so rules cannot loop within one tick.
  • A node with no outgoing edges returns to the initial node straight after its results run. A node marked final stops that FSM.
  • Reconfigurations from a node follow the same editing rules as direct edits (beam-in, keep-out, budget). One that cannot be applied is skipped and logged.
  • An FSM has one initial node, up to 63 other nodes, and up to 64 edges. The proposed limit is 32 FSMs at once.

Result kinds of an FSM node

  • Score change.
  • Sound effect, chosen from a fixed library.
  • Visuals from a fixed library: set a lamp, pulse flashers, particle burst at a component.
  • On-screen text: a short message in the side panel or floating at a component.
  • Arena reconfiguration: create, move, resize or delete components, or change a setting (open a gate, flip a diverter, switch a magnet, raise a post, reset a drop-target bank).
  • Ball actions: add a ball (multiball), release a held ball, start a ball save, award an extra ball.
  • Raise a signal that other FSMs can listen for, which lets rules be composed.

The quest example of Figure 2

  • From the initial node, the edge "drop target in bank A drops, count 3" enters "Bank down": light the ramp lamp, text RAMP IS LIT.
  • From "Bank down", the edge "track R completed" enters "Jackpot": +50,000 score, flashers, fanfare, reset bank A. Then back to the initial node.
  • From "Bank down", the edge "timeout 20 s" enters "Missed": lamp off, text TOO SLOW, reset bank A. Then back to the initial node.

Flipper player

  • Sees: every ball's position, speed and level. Both flippers' angles. The plunger state. Every component (type, place, size, rotation, lamp state, hologram or solid). The current messages. Its score, each score change with the component that caused it, the ball number, the clock and the tilt warnings.
  • Does not see: the FSMs themselves.
  • Does: left flipper up or down, right flipper up or down, plunger pull or release, nudge left, right or up.

Arena player

  • Sees: the whole arena state (every component and setting, budget left). Every ball's position, speed and level. The flippers' actuation state (up, down, moving, angle). The plunger state. Every FSM's current node. The score, the ball number and the clock. Which of its edits were rejected.
  • Does: create, move, rotate, resize and delete components. Change settings. Create and delete FSMs, add nodes and edges, reset an FSM. Raise script signals.

Look and sound

  • Side panel, top to bottom: title plate, ball number, large score readout, two message boxes (upper: current quest text, lower: last award or status), clock. Messages are short, capitals, terse ("RAMP IS LIT", "JACKPOT 50,000").
  • View: the classic 4:3 composition, with the table on the left (about two thirds) and the side panel on the right.
  • Palette and materials: a deep navy and purple starfield playfield with planets. Chrome rails and wireforms. Grey metal and smoked-plastic ramps. Glowing lamps in yellow, red, green and blue. A chrome ball. Polyworld's normal look is toon-shaded low-poly1.
  • Edits: beam-in holograms are cyan wireframe ghosts with a short shimmer, and fade-outs dissolve. This fits the theme and makes edits readable.
  • Levels: they read by height, cast shadow and material. Level 2 track is chrome wire, and level 3 track is tinted clear plastic. Balls on raised track are drawn above the playfield parts that they pass over.
  • Sound effects: spring-and-whoosh launch, bright bumper pings, mechanical flipper clacks, target thunks, sci-fi sweeps for holes, beam-in and jackpots. The fixed sound library for FSM nodes is proposed at about 32 cues.

Appendix H · Notes for the builder

This appendix is for the engineer or coding agent that builds the game. Read the whole document first. The Decision Record (Appendix I) lists what is settled, so do not reopen those points.

Who decides what

  • The project owner decides how the game looks, feels and behaves. Bring only such questions to the owner.
  • The builder makes every implementation decision. The guidelines are minimalism, simplicity and no over-engineering. Do not ask the owner implementation questions.
  • The numbers in Appendix A and Appendix C are starting values for playtest.

Starting points

  • Engine. Polyworld, github.com/softmax-universe/polyworld, read at commit be1706dc. Make a standalone fork, as Paintbot did (softmax-universe/paintbot-pw). The smallest full example game is games/heartleaf/. Its bots.nim shows how a game gives observations and actions to a BASIC script (with src/polyworld/policyhosts.nim). Replays are in src/polyworld/tapes.nim, audio rules in docs/audio.md, and the repeatability test in tests/determinism.nim. The browser build uses -d:emscripten.
  • BASIC. Bassy, github.com/treeform/bassy.
  • Gamepad. paddy, github.com/treeform/paddy.
  • AIDE contract. Entry point softmax.com/docs/aide. Version 1.0.0 has the protocol rules, the AsyncAPI specification and example exchanges (protocol.md, asyncapi.json and examples.json under https://softmax.com/docs/static/aide/1.0.0/). Appendix D is the list of what the page must do.
  • Softmax client code. The wire client is the package @softmax-universe/aide, published on GitHub Packages. The sign-in flow, the encrypted cookie and the WebSocket pass-through are the server half of the Softmax Cogent SDK. Softmax keeps the list of allowed cogent sites.
  • Assets. Appendix F. Start the licence record with the first asset that is added.

Suggested build order

  1. Ball physics, flippers, plunger and nudge on a fixed table, with replays that match. This carries the feel of the game and is the largest risk.
  2. Components, the editing rules and the build budget.
  3. Track and the three levels.
  4. FSM rules and their results.
  5. The BASIC interfaces for both players, the house Arena script and the demo Flipper script.
  6. Look and sound, with the assets and the licence record.
  7. The browser build, gamepad input, the public site on Vercel, and signed-out play.
  8. The cogent connection, to the list in Appendix D.

Steps 1 to 7 need nothing from Softmax. The acceptance checks are the "design works when" list in section 11.

Still to write

  • The Arena BASIC guide: the reference that teaches the cogent the observation records, the action calls, the component and event names, and the first-line directive. It must fit in 64,000 characters.
  • The House table layout and its rule set.
  • The exact names and fields of the BASIC interfaces and of the Episode summary.
  • The shape and art of each component.

Needs someone at Softmax

  • Add the address of the site to the allowed cogent sites list.
  • Confirm that a program that runs inside the BASIC virtual machine obeys the "never runs it in the page" rule of the contract.
  • Confirm that the cogent side sends policy.create and policy.update, accepts surface.event, and can be given the Arena BASIC guide.

Not verified

  • Whether @softmax-universe/aide version 0.2.0 handles the policy and surface messages. They entered the contract on 16 November 2026.
  • How a page registers an agent guide name on the cogent side. The design sends the guide in the instructions field instead.
  • The Cogent SDK server half expects a Node upgrade handler. Whether it fits the WebSocket support of Vercel functions without an adapter is not tested, and the time limit of those sockets is not confirmed.
  • Whether the Bassy revision in use supports TYPE records. Its README and the Paintbot guide disagree.

Out of scope for now

  • A score for the Arena player.
  • What the cogent learns from and when it revises, including a fun rating.
  • A live view or live control of the game for the cogent.
  • Leagues, ranking and comparable scores.

Appendix I · Decision Record
D1 · Settled

Seat names are Flipper player and Arena player. "Flipper" replaces "paddle" as the correct pinball term.

D2 · Settled

Scoring is FSM-only. Built-in component scores lost because they duplicate the rule system.

D3 · Settled

Edits beam in with a delay. Instant editing and between-balls-only editing lost (see Alternatives considered, item 2).

D4 · Settled

Pieces never slide. Sliding lost because it lets walls shove the ball.

D5 · Settled

The ball stays on surfaces. Full 3D flight lost for readability and tractability.

D6 · Settled

Fixed single view. A scrolling camera lost because it breaks the reference look.

D7 · Settled

"Flip targets" is read as drop targets plus a toggle target.

D8 · Settled

A human Arena editor is out of scope for now.

D9 · Settled

The look is "in the style of". The table and the names are original.

D10 · Settled

The game runs at 120 steps per second. Polyworld's shared 24 ticks per second lost because a press could land up to 42 ms late.

D11 · Answered question (was Q8)

The game lives in a standalone fork of Polyworld, as Paintbot does. A game folder inside the Polyworld repository lost.

D12 · Settled

The game is a public website on Vercel, not a Softmax-hosted league Coworld. Cloudflare was the other candidate.

D13 · Answered question (was Q1, Q2)

No league, no ranking, and scores are not made comparable. The Arena player's aim is to make the Flipper player have fun and improve. How the Arena player is scored is undecided.

D14 · Settled

The Arena player is the person's cogent, connected through AIDE, and it sends BASIC programs. Pod (container) players were dropped with the Softmax hosting.

D15 · Answered question (was Q3)

Rules can subtract score. The Flipper score floor is 0.

D16 · Answered question (was Q4)

The Flipper player cannot read the FSMs.

D17 · Answered question (was Q6)

Editing limits are configurable table settings with the listed defaults.

D18 · Answered question (was Q7)

Level 1 is the playfield. Levels 2 and 3 are raised.

D19 · Settled

Gamepad input uses the paddy library.

D20 · Settled

Art and sound use CC0 assets where they fit, with a licence record.

D21 · Settled

The Arena program runs in the browser inside the BASIC virtual machine. A server-run game lost (see Alternatives considered, item 7).

D22 · Settled

The cogent decides when a new table program takes effect. The default is the start of the next Episode. The other choices are the next ball and at once. (Was Q9.)

D23 · Deferred

What the cogent learns from, and when it revises, is not designed yet. The fun rating and automatic revision after each Episode were dropped. The page sends a plain Episode summary, and the work now is to follow the AIDE contract. (Was Q10.)

D24 · Settled

People can play without signing in. The house Arena script runs the table, and there is no chat. Sign-in first lost. (Was Q11.)

D25 · Settled

The person does not approve each new table program. The page accepts it and shows a notice. An approval step, and a setting for one, lost. (Was Q12.)

D26 · Settled

A live view of the game for the cogent is not ruled out. It is not built now. The surface messages of the AIDE contract are the likely route.
